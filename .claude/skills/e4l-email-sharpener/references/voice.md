## How he actually writes

Study the two originals at the bottom of this file. What's in them:

- **"Yo [First Name],"** on the student side. **"Hey [First Name],"** on the agency
  side — Chosen's call (2026-09-20), even though his original agency email used "Yo."
  Agency is peer-to-owner; "Yo" reads as talking down to someone who runs something.
- **Numbered beats, three of them.** Each beat: a bold-able header line, then 2–4 short
  lines. Item 3 is always the one they can DO today.
- **Line breaks every one or two sentences.** It reads like texts.
- **Caps for emphasis, sparingly.** YOUR, NUTS, MONTHS, TODAY. One or two per email.
- **Emoji as punctuation, not decoration.** 👀 😵‍💫 😂 🎮 💡 👉 — five or six per
  email, at the end of a line, never in the subject.
- **"The part that excites me / gets me:"** — his pivot into the point.
- **The P.S. (or P.S.S.) is a note from a friend, not a formula.** Chosen's call,
  2026-10-07: sometimes he uses P.S., sometimes P.S.S., and it is **not always a
  question.** It can be a joke, a fact, a piece of advice, a question, or just the
  thing a friend scribbles at the bottom before sealing the envelope. "I gotta
  question:" is one flavor of it, not the rule. A question P.S. still earns the most
  replies, so keep some, but across a batch of eight mix them. A batch where every P.S.
  opens "I gotta question:" reads like a template, which is the opposite of the point.
  A P.S.S. is a second afterthought, used when the first P.S. wasn't the last thing he
  thought of. Never both in every email.
- **Concrete, near, and cheap.** "6 MONTHS. Now 6 minutes." "Any room — 20 minutes."
  "Businesses will PAY you TODAY." Time and money, stated flat.
- **Gaming as the handshake.** "We're still waiting on GTA 6…", "side quest", "Level
  up", "the energy behind." References any casual player gets. Never a mechanic that
  requires a specific game.
- **Plain, warm, a little cocky.** Not corporate, not guru. "Let's see what you cook."

From *Esports Unlocked*, the harder edge that's still him:
- "All longer, wordier ways to say LAZY."
- "Success requires sacrifice. This is non-negotiable."
- "Being a lifelong student is more valuable than a degree from the Ivy Leagues."
- "Everyone works hard, so everyone eats." — the origin of Everybody Eats.
- "Let's Eat."

He'll say the uncomfortable thing and then say "I'm not saying I don't understand."
That's the shape: name the excuse, refuse it, empathize, hand them the move.

## Why a structurally perfect email can still be boring

The first batch through the skill (Sept 21) hit every check and Chosen's read was
"a bit boring." Read those emails and the pattern is obvious in hindsight: they
*explain the news*. Correct beats, correct link, correct CTA — and not one line a
smart marketer couldn't have written. What was missing, every time:

- **A correlation from outside the industry.** "Muse gives one answer" is a fact.
  "The Yellow Pages died because Google gave ten answers instead of three hundred —
  this thing gives one" is a picture. JFSI: the further the source, the harder the
  click.
- **A stance.** "Here's the part your marketing guy won't say out loud" is Chosen.
  "Here's why that matters" is a blog.
- **The reader's Tuesday, not the reader's category.** Not "small business owners" —
  the phone that stopped ringing and nobody heard it stop.
- **Some "me."** A real moment, a real opinion, a real loss. Ask Chosen for one if
  the story bank doesn't have it. Never invent one.
- **The offer as a picture.** "Book a call" is a button. "I'll screen-share the AI and
  show you what it sees when it looks for you" is a reason to click.

Boring is the one failure the fourteen checks don't catch, because check #2 ("would
I want to read this?") is easy to pass when you wrote it. Use the stricter test: is
there a line in here that would make Chosen say "yeah, that's me"?

## What he wants the reader to feel

From his notes: they want to learn; they want to feel *right*; they want to feel like
they're getting a deal. Support what they're passionate about. Believe in them the way
others don't. Forgive them for not knowing — you're the lighthouse, not the judge.
Tap the ego: they want freedom, show someone free; they want comfort, show comfort.
They need to see themselves on the other side of the bridge. Offer self-improvement,
growth, hope. "People want an expert, not a friend. Be an expert."

And the frame: sales happen when the future state is a better state. Name the better
state — more leads, more time, the edge, the first client, the raise that didn't need
permission.

**What to copy from it — the student playbook:**

1. **Subject matter: what the reader's feed is arguing about this week.** A viral game, a
   drop, a gaming/culture moment with a real number attached. Not a survey, not a vendor
   announcement. The reader should already half-know the story before they open.
2. **Have an opinion — two of them.** Pick it apart honestly first (numbered, with real
   heat: "I'm not the police but I'm sayinnnn"), then flip it: "And here's what pisses me
   off most… it's working." Mad at it and impressed by it at the same time is the voice.
3. **Culture and responsibility, said straight.** He'll name what's wrong for the
   community — kids, violence, who profits off the hood — without preaching. One beat,
   then move.
4. **The flip lands on the reader's opportunity.** The gatekeepers lost the keys; AI
   makes one person a whole team. The story is proof the door is open.
5. **The book shows up as his own words, not an ad.** Quote a line from From Xbox to
   Executive (the ❌/✅ contrast pair is a signature), link the title, then the 👉 CTA.
6. **One rule the reader can repeat.** "If you wouldn't put your real name on it, don't
   build it." Bold it.
7. **The P.S. reopens the hook** and invites a one-line reply. "I gotta question:" is his
   phrasing — keep it.
8. **Signature lines:** "Yo", "My boy", "I'm sayinnnn", "pisses me off", "crazy enough",
   "Just be smooth with it", "The gatekeepers just LOST the keys", CAPS on the one word
   that carries the beat, `…` for the pause.

**The one thing the original got wrong, and the rule it taught:** his draft repeated
details that had spread online but didn't check out (mode names, the map, the studio's
city, "made with AI"), and the subject promised five things with three in the body.
Viral stories are exactly where bad facts travel fastest. Every detail gets checked at
the primary source (the Steam store page, SteamDB, the developer's own posts); anything
unconfirmed is either cut or said as "the internet's calling it…". The number in the
subject matches the number in the body.
