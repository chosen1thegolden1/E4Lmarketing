# Email shapes Chosen supplied

Proven structures he wants used. A shape is a skeleton, not a voice — it tells you what
goes in what order, and everything else in this skill still applies: his greeting, his
length, his walls, the one line a copywriter couldn't have written.

**Never fill a shape in as written.** The placeholder copy in these templates is generic
direct-response English. Dropped into an E4L email unchanged it reads like a swipe file,
which is the opposite of why it works.

---

## Shape 1 — GAIN (the offer open)

Added 2026-10-31. This is the **first email of a promo**, the one that opens with the
offer. In the `jfsi.md` escalation it is the open, followed by the story that sells it,
the proof, the worst way of not acting, the FAQ on the last day, and the last-hours
reminder to recent openers only.

### Subject options he gave
- Here's how to [get a benefit]
- The secret of getting [a benefit]
- Want [a benefit]? Read this…
- The truth about [topic or benefit]…
- The quick and easy way to [get a benefit]…

⚠️ **All five sound like a swipe file**, and `sauce.md` bans exactly this register: read it
out loud, and if it sounds like a headline or an infomercial, rewrite it. Keep the *shape*
— a plain promise of gain — and say it the way he would. "The quick and easy way to land
your first client" becomes "One job. One client. 90 days."

### The skeleton
1. **The triple promise.** "How would you like to X, Y and Z?" Three benefits in one
   sentence, concrete.
2. **Name the disbelief.** Say out loud that it sounds unbelievable, because the reader is
   already thinking it. Unaddressed, that thought kills the email.
3. **The loop they're stuck in.** They make progress, a familiar problem hits, back to
   square one. This is where `audiences.md` earns its keep — the loop is segment-specific
   and naming it exactly is what makes the reader feel seen.
4. **"Until now."** The turn.
5. **Introduce the product**, by name, as the thing built for that result.
6. **Link, early.** The template puts a link above the bullets on purpose: the reader who
   is already sold should not have to scroll.
7. **Benefit bullets.** Each one a thing they'll be able to *do*, not a feature.
8. **Near-term and longer-term payoff.** "By next week… and within a few months…"
9. **Close and link again.**

### Adapting it for E4L
- **Length.** His normal ceiling is ~200 words. A promo open may run longer — a $500 ask
  needs more than a $9.95 one — but every added word has to carry weight. 250–280 is the
  realistic window for a backend offer. Never pad to fill the shape.
- **Bullets must be true.** The template invites five, so the temptation is to invent two.
  **Write only the bullets you can source** from the product, the book's framework or the
  story bank, and if that's three, ship three. An invented feature is an invented fact.
- **"And much, much more"** does not go in an E4L email. It is the line that tells a
  reader there is nothing else.
- **No fake near-term payoff.** "By tomorrow you could…" only if that's literally true.
- **One offer.** The shape sells one thing. Don't bolt a second price on.
- **The deadline comes from `docs/PROMOS.md` or it does not exist.**
- **Step 7 of the procedure still applies:** somewhere in here there has to be one line a
  copywriter couldn't have written. In the gain shape it usually belongs in the loop at
  step 3, or inside a bullet as a real moment.

## Shape 2 — the possibility email (student)

**Where it came from:** Chosen, 2026-10-31. As a former Billy Gene student, he knows what
makes these emails land: *"the promise of making money and using these tools to continue
making money or break away from the rat race."* Study notes, Billy lessons and an idea
bank are in `possibility.md`. Five finished examples are in
`docs/emails/STUDENT_POSSIBILITY_SERIES.md`. Read one before writing another.

### The skeleton

1. **A scene they can check tonight.** A real kind of place in their life (the taco truck,
   their barber, the coffee shop) and the gap in it (last post two months ago, no website,
   no video). The reader goes and looks. No invented business.
2. **The flip.** "That's not a sad story. That's an OPENING."
3. **How easy it is now.** One tool, one sentence typed into it, what comes back. Write the
   actual prompt in quotes. It makes the ease real.
4. **The bold rule.** One line they can repeat: "If you can describe it, you can build it."
5. **The money.** What a business really pays **E4L** for this exact work, from
   e4lagency.com that week, then "That's ONE client." Never what the reader will earn.
6. **The life it buys.** From anywhere (the couch, the bus, another country), shown through
   Chosen's real story or the team, never promised.
7. **Belief + timing.** "I believe you can do this," varied every time, and "the door is
   open RIGHT now" as opinion, never as a fake deadline.
8. **The book.** "The book shows you how to pick ONE skill and get paid for it inside 90
   days." Then the 👉 CTA.
9. **P.S.** A question about *their* version ("what's the first spot you'd make one for?"),
   a reply ask, or a joke. Vary it.

### Energy

Billy Gene's: short lines, CAPS on the one word that carries the beat, "here's what's
crazy," and a reader who should feel slightly out of breath by the CTA. Chosen's voice on
top: "Yo," "I'm not even playing," "real talk," the gaming handshake. Five emoji max, none
in the opening line.

### Walls specific to this shape

- **No income claims.** No "you'll make," no "per client per month," no "replace your
  salary." Our prices, a real proof (story bank, Drip screenshot), and the reader's own math.
- **Tool claims stay modest and sourced the week they're written.** "A simple game." "A
  one-page site." "A video ad in minutes." If the tool changed, the email changes.
- **Never invent a scene as if it happened.** "Picture it" and "try this tonight" are fine.
  "Last week a student of mine…" is not, unless Chosen supplied it.
