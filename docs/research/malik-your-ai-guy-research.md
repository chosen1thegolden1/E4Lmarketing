# Malik, "Your AI Guy": Research Brief for the New E4L Marketing Site

**Prepared for:** Chosen (E4L Marketing)
**Date:** October 8, 2026
**Purpose:** Pressure-test the "AI is the new car insurance" theory, size the knowledge gap we can sell into, define the voice and the mascot rules for Malik, and settle money vs. credits before the site build starts.

---

## The short version

1. **Your theory holds, with one correction.** People already treat AI like insurance: they expect it, they do not enjoy it, and they distrust the companies selling it. Half of Americans are more worried than excited about AI, only about a third of Americans say they trust it, and roughly four in five small business owners will not let AI run even basic tasks without a human watching. The correction: unlike insurance, nobody is *forced* to buy AI yet. About a third of non-adopters simply think they do not need it. So the job is not fear. The job is **"I don't want to think about this. I want someone I trust to handle it."** That is the IT-guy feeling you described, and the data backs it.

2. **The average owner does almost no structured research.** They learn from YouTube and social (57%), webinars (49%), and the tools themselves (31%). A third ask ChatGPT how to use AI. Seven in ten say they need more training, and 77% have no system for prompting. Most know exactly two tools: ChatGPT and Gemini. Agents are "somewhat familiar," connectors and MCP are essentially unknown. That gap is the product. E4L's value is translation and operation, not access.

3. **Language: lead with the outcome, let Malik carry the warmth, keep "AI" in the small print.** Research shows the literal word "AI" in product copy lowers purchase intent by lowering *emotional* trust, and 39% of consumers (54% of Gen Z) say heavy AI use makes them trust a brand less. The antidote in the same research is emotional connection. A recurring brand character is the most reliable way to build it: campaigns with one are 37% more likely to grow share, brand-owned characters are about twice as effective as borrowed ones, and only 4% of U.S. ads and roughly 1 in 441 B2B software brands use one. Malik is white space.

4. **Charge in dollars, not credits.** Credit pricing is the one place where AI companies have reliably torched trust (Cursor, Windsurf). About half of buyers call credits opaque, buyers' preference for consumption pricing on core platforms is *falling*, and the top thing B2B buyers want from vendors is pricing transparency. Credits read like insurance fine print, which is the exact feeling you are trying to beat. Flat monthly plans in dollars, with any metered usage shown in units a human understands (minutes, conversations), never abstract credits.

5. **Yes, Malik works on the school side too.** One character, two postures, like the Gecko selling both car and home insurance. Agency Malik is "the guy who handles it." School Malik is "the one who went first." Animated guide characters in learning have a measured positive effect on transfer and retention, and consistency across both sides compounds the asset. Keep "Everybody Eats" school-only, per your existing guardrail.

## Part 1. The theory, tested: "Consumers see AI like car insurance"

### What you said
AI is coming whether people like it or not. They feel about it the way they feel about insurance: necessary, unloved, sold by people they do not trust. The win is to be the company they *do* trust, in the ads and in the booking experience. And people want to talk about results, not technology. They want to feel "this is my team for this."

### What the data says

**It is coming, and people know it.**
- Half of U.S. workers now use AI at work; 13% use it daily and 28% a few times a week or more (Gallup, 2025).
- 73% to 75% of Americans believe AI will reduce the number of jobs over the next decade (Gallup, 2025).
- 68% of small business owners believe AI advances will be good for their business, and 74% are already using or testing AI tools (Bluevine, April 2026, n=942).

**They do not trust it, and they do not trust the people selling it.**
- 50% of U.S. adults say growing AI use makes them more concerned than excited; only 10% are more excited (Pew, 2025, n=5,023).
- Only 32% of Americans trust AI as a technology. Three times as many Americans reject the growing use of AI (49%) as embrace it (17%) (Edelman Trust Barometer, 2025 and Nov. 2025 flash poll).
- Nearly 80% of Americans say they do not trust businesses to use AI responsibly (Gallup, 2025).
- 78% of small business owners do not fully trust AI to handle low-level tasks without oversight; 31% distrust its accuracy; data-security concern rose from 23% to 33% in a year (Bluevine, 2026).

**The insurance parallel is almost literal.**
- When researchers put the phrase "artificial intelligence" in an otherwise identical product description, purchase intent dropped. The mechanism was lower *emotional* trust, and the effect was strongest for high-risk purchases. The authors' prescribed fix: build emotional connection with the customer (Washington State / Temple, *Journal of Hospitality Marketing & Management*, 2024, n>1,000).
- 39% of consumers now say heavy AI use in a brand's marketing reduces their trust, up from 20% a year earlier. Among Gen Z it is 54% (Fractl / Search Engine Land, 2026, n=1,008).
- In insurance itself: 44% of consumers say they are *less* likely to buy a policy from an insurer that publicly uses AI, and only 20% think it is a good idea for insurers to use it, down from 29% (Insurity, 2025).
- 52% of consumers now trust AI less than humans with their personal data, and 47% have taken a revenue-affecting action against a brand over AI data concerns (Usercentrics / Sapio, March 2026, n=11,000).

**Why GEICO is the right model.** Car insurance is a grudge purchase sold by a distrusted industry. GEICO's agency, The Martin Agency, did two things that map directly onto E4L. First, it refused to sell on fear when the whole category ran on fear. Second, it gave the brand a calm, likeable face (the Gecko) and one concrete, hedged outcome promise ("15 minutes could save you 15% or more"). The 15 and the 15 were chosen in research because they sounded small enough to believe and big enough to matter. System1's ad testing still places Gecko spots in the top 7% of all U.S. insurance ads, and ranks the Gecko among the most enduring "fluent devices" in American advertising. The lesson is not "be funny." The lesson is: **a friendly, consistent character plus a plain outcome promise beats fear and feature talk in a low-trust category.**

### The one correction

Insurance is mandated. AI is not, and many owners have not felt the pressure yet:
- 35% of owners who do not use AI say their business simply does not need it (Fora Financial, 2026).
- 67% of SMBs said AI would not impact their business in 2026 (Thryv, Dec. 2025).
- Most owners report little or no pressure to adopt (Small Business Expo, 2026, n=521).
- Where fear of falling behind does drive adoption, it is at large companies (Grant Thornton, 2026, n=950 leaders). At the small end, the biggest AI-related fear among the owners who have one is that a competitor is using it better (FreshBooks, Sept. 2026).

So FOMO copy ("your competitors are already using AI") will land with a minority and annoy the rest. The emotional job to be done is relief, not urgency: *someone competent I like is handling this, and I get the result without becoming an AI expert.*

### "This is my team for this" is a real buying motive
- Nearly 1 in 5 B2B buyers say their single biggest turn-off is vendors acting like vendors instead of partners (Aberdeen).
- LinkedIn's B2B Institute, with Bain, found that the "hidden buyers" in a company respond less to rational product messaging and more to reassurance that you are a safe, trusted partner that companies like theirs already work with. Relatability beat cheapest and biggest.
- 94% of B2B marketers rate trust-building as the most important factor in brand success (LinkedIn / Ipsos, 2025).
- The IT-company analogy has a warning in it: 94% of clients with a dedicated managed service provider feel protected, yet 58% plan to switch within three years (WatchGuard). The reasons are responsiveness and communication, not expertise. Trust in this category is earned by *how it feels to work with you*, which is why the booking and onboarding experience matters as much as the ad.

## Part 3. Language study: confident millennial swag, intentional Malik

### What the evidence allows and forbids

**Humor works in B2B, with conditions.** A 2025 *Journal of Business Research* study found humor raises attitudes toward both the ad and the brand. It weakens when the joke is unrelated to the product and when the buyer already has a relationship with the vendor. It works best when buyers have time to decide. A Magna / LinkedIn study cited in that paper found over 60% of B2B buyers see little humor or emotion in the ads they get, and those who find an ad funny report a 28% increase in brand connection. Translation: humor is open territory in this category, but it has to be *about the problem* (the missed call, the inbox, the "I'll learn AI this weekend" that never happens), and it should cool down once someone is a client.

**A recurring character is the strongest tool you have.**
- Campaigns with a "fluent device" (a recurring character or line) were 37% more likely to grow market share, 27% more likely to gain customers, and 30% more likely to grow profit (System1 / IPA Databank).
- Brand-owned characters were nearly twice as effective at driving share growth as borrowed celebrities (Orlando Wood, IPA).
- Characters extend digital dwell time by as much as 50% (System1 / Lumen).
- Use has collapsed: 4% of U.S. ads use a recurring character, and about 1 in 441 B2B software companies has a mascot (Louis Grenier's B2B mascot census). The field is empty.
- The counter-argument from B2B marketers is that mascots feel "not serious enough" for enterprise buyers. Your buyer is not enterprise. Your buyer is a local owner who learns from YouTube. That buyer is exactly who the Gecko was built for.

**Trust comes from authenticity, and authenticity is cultural.**
- 63% of Gen Z would abandon a brand they felt was inauthentic, versus 53% to 59% of older groups (vendor-cited consumer data).
- About half of Gen Z and Millennial consumers trust brands more when Black consumers are authentically reflected (Urban One "Cultural ROI" study, 2025, commissioned).
- 76% of Americans say it is very important to be able to tell whether content is made by AI or by people (Pew, 2025). Malik is AI-generated. Do not hide it, and do not lead with it. He is a character, like the Gecko is a character. The honesty lives in a one-line "About Malik" note, not in the headline.

**The word "AI" is a trust tax.** Combine the WSU purchase-intent study with Fractl's 39% and the Insurity 44%, and the rule is simple: the outcome is the headline, the human relationship is the sub-headline, and "AI" is the *how*, mentioned plainly and late. Your existing tagline wall ("AI Marketing Made Easy.") is fine as a category label because "Made Easy" is the promise. Everything under it should talk results.

### The E4L voice, defined

**Position:** the friend who already figured AI out and is handling it for you. Not a vendor, not a guru, not a robot. "Your AI Guy."

**Tone dials (0 to 10):**
- Confidence: 8. We know what we're doing and we say so without hedging every sentence.
- Warmth: 8. We are on your side. We never make the reader feel behind.
- Humor: 5. Dry, situational, about the problem. Never about the client. Never slapstick on service pages.
- Jargon: 2. Every technical term gets a one-line plain translation within arm's reach.
- Hype: 1. No "revolutionary," no "game-changing," no "unlock." Those are the words the 39% are tired of.
- Cultural fluency: real, not performed. Write the way Chosen talks when he is explaining something to a friend who owns a barbershop. Rhythm and specificity carry the swag. Slang that has to be looked up does not.

**Seven writing rules**
1. **Outcome first, mechanism second.** "Your phone gets answered. Every call, every time." Then: "Malik's voice agent picks up in two rings and books the job."
2. **One concrete number per promise, hedged honestly.** GEICO's "15 minutes" and "15% or more" is the template. Your Revenue Leak Scorecard already does this. Keep the "directional estimate" disclaimer; it reads as honesty, which is the whole brand.
3. **Say "you" more than "we," and "we" more than "AI."** Count it on every page.
4. **Translate every term in the same breath.** "Connectors (that's how the AI gets into your calendar and inbox without you copy-pasting)."
5. **Short sentences for confidence. One long one for warmth.** Vary the rhythm so it sounds like a person.
6. **Never promise results you cannot show.** This is already your guardrail. It is also the top trust driver in every dataset above.
7. **The reader is never the joke.** Malik can be exasperated at missed calls, spam inboxes, and "I'll do it Monday." He is never exasperated at the owner.

### Malik: the character bible

**Who he is.** A young, sharp, likeable guy in a black hoodie with a lightning-bolt fade and big expressive eyes. In the school ads he has a full life: a boss, a girlfriend (Janee), his mom (Brenda), two friends. That history is an asset. It means he is a *person* with a story, not a logo with a face, which is what makes a character trustworthy over time.

**What he is for.** He is the emotional-connection fix the research prescribes. He lowers the stakes of a scary category. He is the "guy" in "I've got a guy for that."

**Gecko rules, applied to Malik**
- **He is the spokesperson, not the comedian.** The situations are funny. He is calm. The Gecko's whole act is being the reasonable one in an unreasonable world.
- **He is always on the customer's side.** He never sells *at* the reader. He explains, reassures, points.
- **He has one job per scene.** One line, one point, one gesture.
- **He is consistent.** Same outfit, same voice, same values, across the agency site, the school, ads, and emails. Consistency is what the System1 data is actually measuring.
- **He can lean on things.** Literally: the nav bar, the edge of a section, a CTA button, the corner of the booking form. The "lean" is his pose. It says relaxed confidence. Build a lean library of six to eight poses and reuse them everywhere.
- **He never overpromises.** If a number is an estimate, Malik says so. That is the brand.

**Two postures, one character**

| | Agency Malik | School Malik |
|---|---|---|
| Role | "Your AI Guy." The one who handles it. | The one who went first. The upperclassman. |
| Stance | Leaning, pointing, thumbs-up on confirmation | Standing, at a whiteboard, walking ahead |
| Register | Reassuring, dry, concrete | Encouraging, direct, a little more fire |
| Signature line | "I got this one." | "Everybody eats. Let's go." (school-only) |
| What he never does | Sell fear, use jargon, mock the owner | Talk down, promise income, skip the work |

**Malik's voice: do and don't**
- Do: "Say less." "I got you." "Here's the move." "Real talk:" (sparingly). Plain contractions. Direct address.
- Don't: "Leverage." "Revolutionary." "Unlock." "Seamless." "Game-changing." "In today's fast-paced world." Any sentence that starts with "AI-powered."

**Sample lines for the build**
- Hero: *"You don't need to learn AI. You need a guy."* Sub: *"Malik and the E4L team set it up, run it, and show you the numbers. You run your business."*
- Services intro: *"Pick the result. We'll handle the robots."*
- Voice AI card: *"Your phone gets answered. Every call, even the 7pm one."*
- Follow-up automation card: *"Leads get a reply in 60 seconds. Not 'when you get to it.'"*
- Reporting card: *"Every month you get a number. In dollars and hours, not in vibes."*
- Trust block: *"Nothing runs without a human who can stop it. Your data stays yours. Ask me anything about that. Seriously."*
- Ask Malik glossary, "connectors": *"How the AI gets into your calendar, inbox, and CRM without you copy-pasting. Think of it as giving the new hire the keys."*
- Booking page: *"20 minutes. No pitch deck. We look at where money is leaking and whether we can plug it. If we can't, I'll tell you."*
- Booking confirmation: *"Locked in. I'll have your scorecard pulled up when we start."*
- 404: *"That page left. I didn't. Here's the way back."*
- School: *"I didn't have a guy. So I became one. Here's exactly how."*

## Part 4. Money or credits (agency side)

### What the market learned the hard way
- The highest-profile AI pricing failures of the last two years were credit systems. Cursor's move from flat pricing to credits produced allotments that vanished after a few complex prompts and a public CEO apology. Windsurf's own postmortem said a flat credit rate "led users to be scared of asking quick questions." Credit anxiety changes behavior; it makes customers use the product less.
- About half of buyers in a pricing-consultant poll said credit pricing is opaque and hard to predict, rising to 60% in some segments. The people most likely to be buying credit-priced AI are the ones most likely to find it opaque (Steven Forth, Ibbaka; informal poll).
- The core complaint, per Metronome's analysis: buyers do not understand what a credit translates to in value. Finance teams like credits. Customers do not. Expirations and surprise overages erode trust.
- "Credit fatigue" is now a procurement term: buyers juggling multiple vendors' credit systems push for simpler, transparent structures.
- Among 830 IT decision-makers, preference for consumption pricing on *core* platforms fell 5.8 points to 30.1% as buyers retreated toward predictability. For generative-AI *features* it rose to 42.9% (Futurum, 2026). Buyers will accept metering for a clearly AI-shaped add-on; they want the main relationship to be predictable.
- Pricing transparency is the number-one change B2B tech buyers want from vendors (45%, TrustRadius).
- Credits are also rarer than the noise suggests: one analysis of 800 AI companies found 13% use credits as the primary metric.

### Through your own lens
You are selling trust to people who feel about AI the way they feel about insurance. Credits are the AI equivalent of insurance fine print: an abstract unit, a conversion rate the customer cannot see, and a nagging feeling they are being metered. It is the exact sensation the whole brand is built to remove.

### Recommendation
**Price in dollars. Flat monthly plans named after the outcome, not the technology.** Your existing tier names (Recover, Accelerate, Own) are already outcome-shaped. Keep that structure.

- **Include AI usage in the plan with a fair-use allowance** ("up to 1,500 voice minutes a month; most clients use under 600"). If a client blows through it, bill the overage in a *human* unit (minutes, conversations), shown in dollars on the invoice, never in credits. This is what the GoHighLevel stack already supports: AI Employee is a flat per-sub-account fee, with usage drawn from a wallet that you can rebill at a markup. Flat-rate the client; absorb the wallet math yourself.
- **Show the number before they ask.** A plain "How pricing works" page with Malik explaining it in three sentences beats a hidden price. Your current guardrail is "no pricing in automated assets, pricing happens on the Game Plan Call." The research says 45% of buyers want transparency most, and the brand promise is honesty. The middle path: publish *how* pricing works and a "plans start at" floor, keep the exact quote on the call. That is your decision; the data leans toward showing more, not less.
- **Never use the word "credits" on a client-facing surface.** If you need an internal unit for ops, fine. The client sees dollars, minutes, and conversations.
- **One exception worth testing later:** a self-serve, low-touch product (a Malik voice agent a client sets up themselves) could carry simple per-minute pricing, because buyers accept metering for an obviously AI-shaped feature. Even then: minutes, not credits.

## Part 5. Malik as the confident leader for the school side

**It works, and it compounds.**
- Animated guide characters in learning ("pedagogical agents") have a measured positive effect: g = .45 on transfer and g = .23 on retention across Mayer and Fiorella's meta-analysis, with a 43-study review finding a small but significant learning effect. The effect depends on the character's appearance and role, which argues for a consistent, well-designed Malik rather than a generic avatar.
- Duolingo's owl is the case study for a character woven through every surface (lessons, notifications, email, social). Nobody has isolated the owl's contribution from streaks and leagues, but the integration pattern is the lesson: Malik should be in the course UI, the reminder emails, and the community, not only the ads.
- The System1 consistency finding is the compounding argument. Every school ad with Malik builds recognition the agency site inherits, and every agency touchpoint builds recognition the school inherits. One character across both sides is worth more than two characters.

**The risk, and how to manage it.** The same face selling to a business owner and leading a student could feel confused if the *values* differ. They do not need to. The Gecko sells car insurance and home insurance with the same personality. Malik's values are identical on both sides (honest, competent, on your side, no hype). Only the posture changes: on the agency side he leans and handles it; on the school side he stands and shows the way. "Everybody Eats" stays school-only, "AI Marketing Made Easy" stays client-only, exactly as your guardrails already say.

**Practical note on the asset.** Your Higgsfield reference for Malik (element "Malik," described as "Main character of the E4L Story") shows him mid-story and emotional. The site needs a **confident expression set**: relaxed lean, pointing to a CTA, thumbs-up, thinking, headset-on, arms-crossed-smiling, and a "peeking in from the edge" pose, all on transparent backgrounds at two sizes. That is a one-session generation job using the existing element and should be the first production task before the site build.

## Decisions this brief makes for the website build

1. **Hero promise:** outcome-led, Malik present, "AI" in the sub-copy. Working line: "You don't need to learn AI. You need a guy."
2. **Site structure:** results-first service cards, an "Ask Malik" interactive glossary, a trust block (oversight, data, honesty), the Revenue Leak Scorecard as the front door, and a booking flow where Malik guides each step.
3. **Malik behavior:** a lean library of six to eight poses, scroll-aware placement (he leans on the nav, peeks at section edges, points at the CTA), reacts on hover, and speaks in one-line tooltips. Reduced-motion fallback is a static lean.
4. **Voice:** the seven rules above, the do/don't list, and the tone dials become the copy standard for the site, ads, and emails.
5. **Pricing:** dollars, flat plans, human units for overage, a "how pricing works" page. No credits anywhere client-facing.
6. **School side:** same Malik, leader posture, "Everybody Eats" school-only.
7. **Transparency note:** one line on the About page that Malik is an animated character created with AI tools, because 76% of people say they want to be able to tell.

## What I would do next, in order
1. Generate Malik's confident expression set from the existing Higgsfield element (transparent PNGs, two sizes).
2. Write the full copy deck for the site using the rules above (hero, five service cards, trust block, glossary entries, booking flow, confirmation, 404, school landing).
3. Build the site: interactive, mobile-first, Malik-aware layout, on the existing black and gold brand system, Rethink Sans.
4. Build the "How pricing works" page copy for your review before it goes live, since it touches an existing guardrail.
